## Research implications
- The requested platform should separate a local privacy control plane from optional external model execution. The safe default is to keep raw PII, secrets, regulated documents, identity maps, and audit evidence inside Uzbek-controlled infrastructure.
- Masking needs to cover not only PII but also credentials/secrets, financial identifiers, health data, confidential business data, source code/IP, prompt-injection content, and tool/action parameters.
- Reversible pseudonymization/tokenization is appropriate only when an application needs the final answer to refer back to the original entity; irreversible redaction/generalization is safer for analytics and broad prompts.
- Claims about external providers' retention, training use, regional processing, and contractual controls must be verified provider-by-provider and tenant-by-tenant.

## Verified Uzbek official-source findings

### Personal-data law (LexUZ, Law No. ZRU-547, current page reviewed Aug 11, 2026)
The official LexUZ text defines personal data as information on electronic, paper, or other media that relates to a natural person or enables identification. It defines processing broadly, including collection, systematization, storage, use, provision, dissemination, transfer, depersonalization, and destruction. The law’s principles include legality, confidentiality, protection, and security.

Article 15 defines cross-border transfer as transfer outside Uzbekistan and allows it to adequate-protection states; transfer to states without adequate protection may occur with subject consent, for specified public-interest/security reasons, or under international treaties. Article 16 defines depersonalization as making attribution to a specific subject impossible.

The current LexUZ page includes a March 26, 2026 amendment (Law No. ZRU-1125) to Article 27-1. The current text requires storage inside Uzbekistan for biometric and genetic data and telecom-user personal data. Other personal data may be stored and processed outside Uzbekistan if one of several conditions is met: the foreign state is recognized as providing adequate protection; the operator follows approved standard contractual clauses or binding corporate rules; or the operator follows listed international data-management/storage standards. Therefore, the earlier blanket claim that all Uzbek citizens’ personal data must always remain on servers physically inside Uzbekistan is too broad for the current text and must be qualified. A sovereign design remains the safest default for government/bank restricted workloads, but cross-border processing is not automatically prohibited for every data category under the current Article 27-1 wording.

Article 20 links mandatory local-storage databases to registration in the State Register; Articles 27 and 28 require legal, organizational, and technical safeguards and confidentiality. Articles 31 and related provisions create duties for owners/operators, including registration, protection measures, and written notice in specified third-party-transfer situations. These are procurement and governance requirements, not merely cryptographic requirements.

### National AI strategy (LexUZ, Resolution RP-358, approved Oct 14, 2024; entry into force Oct 17, 2024)
The official English page describes a Strategy for the Development of Artificial Intelligence Technologies until 2030. It sets targets including AI software/services volume of USD 1.5 billion, 10% AI-based services via the Unified Interactive Public Services Portal, 10 AI research laboratories and high-performance computing servers, and a top-50 Government AI Readiness Index position. It identifies banking/finance, tax/customs, healthcare, agriculture, energy, digital government, education, culture, and tourism as priority sectors. It also calls for legal and ethical frameworks, data security, technical infrastructure for AI/data processing, large-data databases, HPC capacity, and skills development.

Implication: the proposed routing/masking platform aligns directly with official policy priorities, especially banking/finance, digital government, technical infrastructure, data security, and AI skills. The strategy supports the business case but does not itself authorize cross-border sharing of regulated data or prove any vendor/product claims.

### Bank secrecy (official LexUZ, Law No. 530-II, current page reviewed Aug 11, 2026)
The official LexUZ act defines bank secrecy broadly: client transactions, accounts and deposits; information about the client obtained in connection with banking services; client assets held in bank safes/premises; interbank operations performed for or on behalf of the client; and certain related information. It defines disclosure as publication, distribution, oral/written communication, or otherwise making bank-secrecy information available to third parties, including through storage failures.

The act permits disclosure to third parties in specified cases, including necessary legal, accounting, audit, information, and consulting services, provided the service is necessary and the recipients are bound by the statutory confidentiality obligations. It separately states that banks must guarantee protection and adopt organizational and technical measures, including information-security and cybersecurity measures, for proper storage. This supports a design in which an AI platform is treated as a tightly controlled service provider/processor with contractual confidentiality, least-privilege access, local or approved processing, and auditable controls; the law does not provide a blanket authorization to send bank-secret data to any external model API.

## Authoritative security and AI-risk references

### NIST SP 800-122
NIST’s official publication page identifies SP 800-122 as the Guide to Protecting the Confidentiality of Personally Identifiable Information. Its abstract says PII should be protected from inappropriate access, use, and disclosure; the guide supports context-based identification of PII, determining an appropriate protection level, safeguards, and incident response. It maps to access control, audit/accountability, identification/authentication, media protection, planning, risk assessment, and system/communications protection. It is a 2010 U.S. federal guide, not Uzbekistan law, so it should be used as an engineering reference rather than a legal compliance claim.

### NIST AI RMF
NIST’s official page states that the AI Risk Management Framework is voluntary and intended to incorporate trustworthiness considerations into the design, development, use, and evaluation of AI systems. It identifies four core functions: Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage. The page also confirms the July 26, 2024 release of NIST AI 600-1, the Generative AI Profile, which addresses unique generative-AI risks. The framework is useful for the proposed platform’s risk register, control ownership, evaluation gates, and operating model; it is not a certification or a substitute for Uzbek regulatory approval.

### OWASP AI-agent security guidance
OWASP’s AI Agent Security Cheat Sheet identifies risks including direct and indirect prompt injection, tool abuse and privilege escalation, data exfiltration, memory poisoning, goal hijacking, excessive autonomy, high-impact action abuse, approval manipulation, cascading failures, malicious AI-console configuration, and denial-of-wallet. Its recommended controls include least-privilege tool sets, per-tool read/write/resource scoping, explicit authorization for sensitive tools, isolated execution, human confirmation for high-impact operations, and careful handling of secrets and outputs. The proposed platform should implement these as policy-enforced runtime controls, not as prompt-only instructions.

### OWASP RAG security guidance
OWASP’s RAG Security Cheat Sheet states that RAG redistributes rather than removes risk across ingestion, embedding, vector storage, retrieval, generation, and downstream agents. It recommends document hashing and integrity checks, provenance tracking, scanning for hidden instructions and invisible Unicode, trusted-source allowlists and approval workflows, access-control metadata on every vector chunk, retrieval-time authorization, tenant isolation, and retrieval logging. It explicitly supports the design decision to enforce ACL/classification filters before vector results enter the model context, and to re-evaluate permissions when source-document access changes.

### LiteLLM routing documentation
LiteLLM’s official routing page documents multi-deployment load balancing, queueing/prioritization, cooldowns, fallbacks, timeouts, and fixed/exponential-backoff retries across providers. It documents Redis support for cooldown and usage tracking in production, multiple routing strategies (weighted pick, rate-limit aware, latency-based, least-busy, custom, and lowest-cost), grouped routing policies, and normalized endpoints for chat completions, embeddings, and image generation. These are strong candidates for the provider-adapter/routing substrate, but sensitive-data policy enforcement, tenant isolation, Uzbek legal controls, and the mapping vault must remain platform-owned rather than delegated to a generic router.

### Presidio documentation
The current Presidio documentation describes fast identification and anonymization for sensitive entities in text and images, with custom/predefined recognizers using NER, regular expressions, rules, checksums, and contextual signals. It offers analyzers, anonymizers, image redaction with OCR, structured-data support, and deployment through Python/PySpark, Docker, and Kubernetes. Crucially, the documentation warns that automated detection cannot guarantee discovery of all sensitive information and that additional protections are required. Presidio is therefore a useful baseline detector/operator library, not a complete sovereign masking assurance system; it must be extended with Uzbek PINFL/passport/TIN/bank patterns, multilingual NER, secrets/IP detectors, policy thresholds, fail-closed behavior, and adversarial evaluation.

### Portcullis academic privacy gateway
The AAAI publication page and extracted abstract confirm Portcullis is a 2025 AAAI paper on a privacy-preserving gateway for third-party LLM inference. The paper describes parallel substitution/anonymization, secure interaction with LLMs, response reconstruction, encrypted-memory processing, and attestation. Its evaluation reports startup scaling and runtime masking/unmasking results on PII and Enron Email datasets, including a reported 96x speedup over Hide-and-Seek for masking/unmasking and improved/equal error rates in the tested setting. These are paper-specific experimental results, not a guarantee for Uzbek/Russian financial and government data or production deployment. The design is a useful reference for a confidential masking boundary and attested execution, but the report should label the numbers as study results and call for local benchmarking.

### Zero trust, FPE, and differential privacy fact-checks
NIST SP 800-207’s official abstract says zero trust moves defense away from static network perimeters toward users, assets, and resources; it assumes no implicit trust based only on network location or ownership, and requires authentication and authorization before access to enterprise resources. This supports mTLS/identity-aware policy enforcement, but NIST does not prescribe JWT specifically and does not itself create an Uzbek compliance requirement.

NIST SP 800-38G (2016, updated Aug. 4, 2016) specifies FF1 and FF3 methods for format-preserving encryption using an approved symmetric-key block cipher. A 2019 draft revision exists; the final page reviewed here is the 2016 FF1/FF3 publication. The report should not present the draft FF3-1 wording as the final current publication without separately citing the draft/revision status. FPE preserves format but is not a substitute for tokenization, vault isolation, key governance, or access control; small domains and poor key management create risks.

NIST SP 800-226 (final March 2025) describes differential privacy as a mathematical framework that quantifies privacy loss when data appears in a dataset and highlights practical privacy hazards. It is appropriate for aggregate analytics or statistical release, not as a default prompt-masking technique for transactional LLM calls. Synthetic data also does not automatically provide formal privacy unless its disclosure risk and privacy guarantees are evaluated.

Correction to the parallel research: Uzbekistan’s current Article 27-1 text, as read directly from LexUZ, does not support the blanket statement that all Uzbek citizens’ personal data must be stored on physical servers inside Uzbekistan. The final report will use the more precise current text and recommend domestic processing for restricted government/bank workloads regardless of the narrower mandatory-localization categories.

### AWS Bedrock Guardrails
AWS documentation states that Bedrock Guardrails can detect/filter undesirable content in user inputs and model responses, mask or block sensitive information such as PII using probabilistic entity detection and custom regex, filter prompt attacks and denied topics, and perform contextual-grounding checks for RAG. It can be applied during model invocation or through an ApplyGuardrail API. The page notes that reasoning content blocks are excluded from these stated protections. This is a useful defense-in-depth reference, but it is provider-specific and does not replace a local masking boundary or authorization-aware retrieval layer.

### Managed and open gateway comparisons
Microsoft Learn documents Azure API Management’s AI gateway as an API-gateway capability for securing, scaling, monitoring, and governing AI models, agents, and tools. It supports multiple provider schemas and self-hosted endpoints, and a unified model API preview that exposes multiple backends through a single OpenAI-compatible endpoint with format translation and shared governance policies. It also documents authentication/authorization, load balancing, token quotas, content-safety policy integration, semantic caching, and token telemetry. This validates the control-plane pattern, but Azure’s managed boundary is not automatically a sovereign Uzbek deployment.

Portkey’s official documentation lists a universal API, semantic/simple caching, MCP support, fallbacks, conditional routing, multimodality, retries, circuit breakers, load balancing, canary testing, timeouts, budget/rate limits, custom hosts for local models, and open-source/self-hosting options. The page uses the marketing phrase “world’s fastest,” which is not treated as fact. Portkey is a useful feature reference and optional component; it is not evidence that PII masking or Uzbekistan compliance is solved.

### Uzbekistan cybersecurity law
The earlier LexUZ URL `https://lex.uz/docs/6055568` returned 404. The current official English source is `https://lex.uz/en/docs/6997403` for Law No. LRU-764 of April 15, 2022, effective July 17, 2022. The page identifies the State Security Service as the authorized state body for cybersecurity and describes powers including control, incident investigation, critical-information-infrastructure registration, cybersecurity requirements, certification/attestation mechanisms, licensing of cryptographic-protection activity, inspections, and mandatory compliance with its instructions. The law has dedicated chapters on cybersecurity incidents and critical-information-infrastructure objects.

This supports treating government and banking deployments as potentially subject to national cybersecurity governance and audit, but the final report will avoid asserting that every AI-routing deployment is automatically classified as critical infrastructure. That determination depends on the system’s legal classification and sector-specific rules.
